What Is Scientific Research?

Definition

A systematic and organized process used to generate new knowledge, verify existing knowledge, explain phenomena, or solve problems — using scientific methods and evidence rather than guessing or personal opinion.

Ordinary Knowledge
  • Based on personal experience
  • Relies on opinions and observations
  • Trial and error
  • Hearsay from others
Scientific Knowledge
  • Systematic observation
  • Measurements and experiments
  • Evidence and data analysis
  • Verification and replication
Example

Opinion: "The transformer is hot because the weather is hot."
Scientific statement: "Transformer temperature increased significantly when loading exceeded 90% of rated capacity under high ambient-temperature conditions." — This can be measured, tested, and verified.

The Research Process
1

Select a Topic & Identify the Problem

Choose a meaningful, researchable problem. E.g., distribution transformers are experiencing frequent failures.

2

Review Previous Studies

Understand what has already been done — to avoid duplication and spot research gaps.

3

Define Objectives & Hypotheses

State clear, measurable goals. Formulate a testable hypothesis if appropriate.

4

Select Methodology & Collect Data

Choose the right research approach. Gather measurements, records, and experimental results.

5

Analyze Data & Discuss Results

Apply appropriate statistical methods. Interpret what the numbers mean.

6

Draw Conclusions & Recommendations

Answer the research question based on evidence. Suggest practical next steps.

Characteristics of Good Research
🎯

Objectivity

Conclusions must be based on evidence — not personal beliefs or expectations.

📐

Accuracy

"The measured top-oil temperature reached 92°C" is scientific. "The transformer was hot" is not.

🔁

Reliability & Replicability

The same method under similar conditions should produce consistent results that others can reproduce.

✅

Validity

The method must actually measure what it claims to measure — not a proxy or approximation.

⚖️

Ethical Conduct

No plagiarism, no fabricated data, no falsified results. Proper citation of all sources.

Types of Research
🔬

Basic Research

Expands scientific knowledge without an immediate commercial application.

🔧

Applied Research

Solves a specific practical engineering problem using existing knowledge.

📊

Descriptive Research

Describes an existing phenomenon by collecting and analyzing data without manipulating variables.

⚗️

Experimental Research

Investigates cause-and-effect relationships through controlled experiments.

Qualities of a Good Researcher
Honesty Objectivity Accuracy Patience Curiosity Critical Thinking Creativity Analytical Ability Communication Skills Time Management

Key Takeaway

"Scientific research begins with a clear question or problem, uses an appropriate methodology to investigate it, relies on evidence and data, and leads to conclusions that can be evaluated and verified."

Lecture 2

Scientific Writing & Research Paper Structure

How to structure, write, and present a research paper that communicates clearly and professionally.

Standard Paper Structure
T

Title

Specific, clear, and concise. It tells the reader exactly what is being studied. "Effect of Loading Conditions on the Thermal Performance of Distribution Transformers" — not just "Transformer Study."

A

Abstract

A self-contained mini-paper: Problem → Objective → Method → Results → Conclusion. Usually 150–250 words.

I

Introduction

Moves from broad context → specific problem → research gap → objective. Explains why the study is necessary.

M

Methodology

Detailed enough that another researcher could replicate the study. Describes equipment, procedures, variables, and analysis methods.

R

Results

Presents findings objectively — data, tables, figures. No interpretation here; just what was measured.

D

Discussion

Interprets the results. Explains why they occurred, compares with existing literature, discusses limitations.

C

Conclusion

Directly answers the research objectives. Summarizes findings without introducing new results or excessive detail.

Key Writing Concepts

Research Problem vs. Research Question

Problem: Increased harmonic distortion in industrial power systems may result in additional transformer losses and thermal stress.
Question: How does harmonic distortion level affect transformer temperature under varying load conditions?

Research Gap

An area where existing knowledge is incomplete, inconsistent, or insufficient. Your research fills this gap. A strong introduction clearly identifies the gap before stating objectives.

Hypothesis

Null (H₀): Harmonic distortion has no statistically significant effect on transformer temperature.
Alternative (H₁): Harmonic distortion has a statistically significant effect on transformer temperature.

Literature Review — Synthesis, Not Summary

Weak (just a list)
  • Smith (2022) studied transformers.
  • Ahmed (2023) studied harmonics.
  • Ali (2024) studied power quality.
Strong (synthesized)
  • Compare findings across studies
  • Identify contradictions and gaps
  • Evaluate methodologies used
  • Connect to your own research gap

Results vs. Discussion

Results
  • State what was measured
  • "Temperature increased by 12°C when THD rose from 5% to 20%"
  • Objective — no interpretation
Discussion
  • Explain why it happened
  • "This may be attributed to harmonic-related losses in the windings and core"
  • Compare with literature
Academic Language Tips
Non-scientific
  • "The transformer became very hot"
  • "Loading was too high"
  • "I think solar energy is best"
Scientific
  • "Top-oil temperature reached 92°C"
  • "Loading exceeded 90% of rated capacity"
  • "The PV system reduced consumption by 18%"
Citation — IEEE Style

In-text: Transformer losses increase under harmonic loading [1].
Multiple: Previous studies reported similar findings [1]–[3].
The full reference goes in the reference list at the end of the paper.

Research Ethics
❌

Plagiarism

Using someone else's work or ideas without acknowledgment.

❌

Fabrication

Inventing measurements or experimental results that were never obtained.

❌

Falsification

Changing or manipulating data to obtain a desired conclusion.

❌

Misrepresentation

Presenting results in a misleading way to support an argument.

Key Takeaway

A well-structured paper guides the reader from problem to solution in a logical, evidence-based flow: Title → Abstract → Introduction → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion. Each section has one clear job — do not mix them.

Lecture 3

Statistical Data Analysis

The tools to turn raw numbers into meaningful, defensible scientific conclusions.

Stages of Data Analysis
1

Data Cleaning

Identify and correct incorrect values, outliers, unit errors, and data-entry mistakes. Never delete a suspicious value without investigating it first.

2

Data Organization

Arrange data in a structured table so that patterns and relationships become visible.

3

Descriptive Analysis

Summarize the dataset using mean, median, mode, range, variance, and standard deviation.

4

Inferential Analysis

Use statistical tests (t-tests, ANOVA, regression) to draw conclusions beyond the observed sample.

5

Interpretation

Explain what the results mean in relation to your research questions and hypotheses.

Descriptive Statistics

Mean (Average)

The sum of all values divided by the number of values. As the number of measurements increases, the mean becomes a better estimate of the true value.

Formula x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + ... + xₙ) / n

Median

The middle value when data is arranged in ascending order. For an even number of values, take the average of the two middle values. As n increases, mean ≈ median.

Mode

The most frequently occurring value. A dataset can have one mode, more than one mode, or no mode at all.

Range

The difference between the maximum and minimum values. A smaller range indicates more consistent measurements.

Formula Range = x_max − x_min

Standard Deviation (σ) — Most Important Measure of Spread

Indicates how far measurements tend to deviate from the mean.

Small σ
  • Measurements close to the mean
  • High precision / reliability
Large σ
  • Measurements widely spread
  • Lower precision, investigate why
Example

Group A: 49, 50, 51 — small σ  |  Group B: 30, 50, 70 — large σ
Both have the same mean (50) but very different reliability. The mean alone is not enough.

Variance and Standard Deviation — Formulas
Deviation of each measurement from the mean dᵢ = xᵢ − x̄
Variance v = Σdᵢ² / (n − 1)
Standard Deviation σ = √v = √[ Σdᵢ² / (n − 1) ]

What do these values tell you?

  • High variance / σ → low precision (data is spread out)
  • Low variance / σ → high precision (data is clustered around the mean)
  • The dataset with the smallest σ is the most reliable
Measurement Set Comparison Example
SetSample Values (mm)MeanRangeConfidence
A398, 420, 394, 416, 404 ...408.536Lower

Key Insight

Set B is more reliable than Set A because its spread (range = 7) is much smaller than Set A (range = 36). As you add more measurements (Set C), the mean and median values converge — confirming reliability. Smaller spread = more confidence in the mean.

What Questions Can Data Analysis Answer?
?

Is there a trend in the data?

?

Is there a relationship between variables?

?

Is there a significant difference between groups?

?

Does one variable affect another?

?

Do the results support the research hypothesis?

Key Takeaway

The mean tells you the center. The standard deviation tells you how trustworthy that center is. Always report both — a result without a measure of spread is an incomplete result.

Lecture 4

Data Presentation

How to present your findings visually and clearly — using the right tool for the right data.

Core Principle

Quality Over Quantity

Use only data that are directly pertinent to your conclusions. Do not try to impress readers with how much data you have collected. Quantity is no substitute for quality in presenting technical results.

Three Methods of Presentation
📈

Figures

Best for complex data, trends, and relationships. Graphs, diagrams, and drawings. Preferred when patterns are hard to see in a table.

📋

Tables

Present precise values. Use when exact numbers matter. Tables are the least preferred method — keep them brief and simple.

📝

Text

Narrate and interpret findings. Tables and figures do not replace text — they supplement it.

Rules for Figures
🔢

Numbering

Figures are numbered with Arabic numerals in order of first mention: Figure 1, Figure 2, ...

🏷️

Title Location

Figure titles appear below the figure itself, with a short, concise description.

📍

Placement

Insert figures in the text near their first mention. Always refer to every figure in the text.

📖

Legends

All figures must have legends — a figure that cannot be understood without reading the full paper fails its purpose.

🎨

Types

Graphs (line, bar, scatter), Diagrams (circuit diagrams, flow charts), and Drawings (schematics, illustrations).

Example Usage

"The single-diode model, shown in Fig. 1, has become the most widely used model as it offers a good compromise between simplicity and accuracy."

→ Always cite the figure number in the text before the reader reaches it.

Rules for Tables
🔢

Numbering

Use Roman numerals (Table I, Table II...) unless there are 20+ tables, in which case Arabic numerals are used.

🏷️

Title Location

Table titles appear above the table (opposite to figures!).

📐

Units

Place units under the column title or beside the row title, enclosed in parentheses — e.g., Temperature (°C).

✂️

Keep It Simple

Tables are not a replacement for text — keep them as brief and self-explanatory as possible.

Figure Title
  • Appears below the figure
  • Arabic numerals (Figure 1, 2 ...)
  • Short and concise
Table Title
  • Appears above the table
  • Roman numerals (Table I, II ...)
  • Must be titled if numbered
Symbols & Notation
1

Define each symbol in the text the first time it appears.

2

If many symbols are used, group definitions in a "Symbols" section.

3

When a symbol list exceeds 30 items, place it in an Appendix.

Quick Reference — When to Use What
SituationBest Method
Show a trend over timeLine graph (Figure)
Compare groups side by sideBar chart (Figure)
Show exact numerical valuesTable
Explain a circuit or systemDiagram (Figure)
Data is too complex for a tableFigure
Narrate and interpret findingsText

Key Takeaway

Every figure and table must add value — not just fill space. If a reader can understand your result from the text alone, the figure is unnecessary. If a figure makes a pattern obvious that text would struggle to describe, it is essential.
